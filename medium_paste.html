Inspired by the LeJEPA paper of Klindt, LeCun & Balestriero

Yann LeCun often makes a striking comparison: a house cat, or a four-year-old child, understands the physical world better than even the largest language models. He also points to how quickly a teenager can learn to drive: about twenty hours of practice, compared with the billions of examples used to train self-driving systems. His argument is that predicting the next word, however well, is not enough to understand the world. At AMI Labs, he is pursuing a different approach: a world model, a system that learns the hidden structure behind what it observes. The hope is that these models could help shape the next generation of AI.

But what does it mean for a machine to learn the world's hidden structure? And how would we know it had succeeded? In a May 2026 preprint, David Klindt, Yann LeCun and Randall Balestriero give a mathematical answer under specific conditions. Their method, LeJEPA, learns by bringing its representations of neighbouring moments closer together, while keeping the overall cloud of representations round and Gaussian. They prove that this can recover the hidden variables behind the measurements, even when instruments mix them together in unknown, nonlinear ways. The recovered variables match the originals up to a rotation.

The conditions matter. The hidden variables must be independent and Gaussian. The world must be stationary, meaning its rules do not change while we record it. The model must also be capable of undoing the mixing, and training must reach the true optimum. Two words will be especially important in the examples that follow: Gaussian and stationary.

Those conditions can be hard to picture from a proof alone. So let’s make them concrete. We’ll start with a simple question: is predicting the readings the same as understanding what lies behind them? A fermenting tank gives us a first example: a machine can predict its instrument readings accurately without recovering the hidden quantities inside. Then, in four experiments, we’ll explore what it takes to recover those quantities and which conditions matter in each case.

In every example, we know the answer in advance: every relationship, every hidden quantity and every constant. We keep that information from the method and give it only the instrument readings. Then we compare its reconstruction with the answers we already have. This gives us a direct way to test whether it has recovered the hidden variables.

Part one · Prediction

Two alarms that pay for themselves

Before getting into the theory, let’s start with prediction doing something useful. In a brewery, a simple curve fitted to past batches can catch trouble well before the final lab test. Sometimes the batch itself is going wrong, and the warning comes early enough to act. Other times the batch is fine but an instrument has failed. In that case, the system can flag the instrument instead of sending good beer to waste. Here is how it works.

Picture a 400 hL tank of lager, held at 12 °C and watched for 42 hours. Inside, one reaction drives the process:

C6H12O6 → 2 C2H5OH + 2 CO2sugar becomes alcohol and gas

We know this reaction, and we know the quantities that decide the beer's quality: how much sugar is left, how much alcohol has formed, and how much yeast is still active. Those are the numbers we care about most — yet in this example they are checked in the lab only at the end, before packaging.

During fermentation, measuring them continuously would mean drawing and analysing samples again and again. That is slow, labour-intensive, and can introduce contamination risk. So during the run, these quantities stay hidden. The operator steers the process using easier measurements instead.

What the panel gives us instead are four instruments, each watching a consequence of the chemistry:

On the panel                      What it is physically watching
--------------------------------  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Density (the brewer's “gravity”)  Sugar is heavy, alcohol is light, so the liquid gets lighter as the reaction runs
CO₂ off the blow-off              Gas leaving the tank: the reaction rate, live
Glycol valve opening              How hard the cooling works, because the reaction gives off heat
Haze                              How much yeast is still floating rather than settled

None of them directly reports sugar, alcohol or active yeast. They are all consequences of what is happening inside the tank.

From previous good batches, we build a golden curve for each instrument: the path a normal ferment tends to follow, with a tolerance around it. If a reading leaves that band, something has changed. But what changed — the batch, or the instrument? That is where the second check comes in.

Check one: the golden curve

A brewery already has the raw material for this check: records from batches that finished well. Average those runs, instrument by instrument, and you get a golden curve. The natural variation among those good batches gives us a tolerance band. A new batch inside the band looks familiar; one that stays outside it for an hour is doing something the good batches did not. For the demonstration here, we generated five good runs with ordinary batch-to-batch variation.

Check two: the instruments are not independent

The four instruments are different windows on the same fermentation, so their readings are related. That relationship gives us a second check. Plot density against CO₂ and haze, for example, and the points do not fill the space randomly; they sit close to a plane:

[[ FIGURE 02 — drag medium/figures/fig02.png here ]]

The readings do not fill the space — density sits on a plane.

That plane can be written as density = 1.04 × CO₂ − 0.007 × haze + 1015, with R² = 0.95 in our simulated good batches. In plain language, CO₂ and haze already tell us a great deal about what density should be. We do the same for every instrument, predicting each one from the other three. Now every reading is checked in two ways: against its own history, and against the story told by the other instruments.

There is one complication. If one instrument breaks, its bad reading also contaminates any formula that uses it, so several prediction gaps can appear at once. A single mismatch therefore cannot identify the culprit. To do that, we repeat the calculation while leaving each suspect instrument out. If the densitometer is wrong, the relationships that depend on density fail, while the instruments that never use it can still agree with one another.

Situation one — the tank goes wrong

With both checks running, we ask two simple things: are the readings following the shape of a good batch, and are the instruments still consistent with one another? In the first test batch, something changes at hour 10. The obvious operating settings still look normal. What actually happened in the simulation is that the yeast began settling early, so less active yeast remained suspended to ferment the sugar. No panel instrument measures that cause directly.

[[ FIGURE 03 — drag medium/figures/fig03.png here ]]

Tank A. CO₂ and haze leave their bands at hour 11; density, the last out, at hour 22.

Its effects, however, do show up. CO₂ and haze leave their golden bands at hour 11, the cooling signal follows, and density — often the most familiar fermentation signal — is much later to move. Several channels also stop agreeing with what the others predict. That pattern points to a process problem rather than one bad gauge. The warning comes at hour 11, leaving 31 hours to act; on the density chart alone one could not be confident until around hour 28. So the system warns early that the batch is drifting, even though it still cannot tell us the hidden cause: something is wrong, not the yeast has settled.

Situation two — the instrument goes wrong

In the second test batch, the fermentation itself is fine, but at hour 10 the densitometer begins a slow drift, like a sensor gradually fouling. Here the two checks separate: the process stays normal while one instrument slowly stops agreeing with the rest.

[[ FIGURE 04 — drag medium/figures/fig04.png here ]]

Tank B. The densitometer reads one curve; the other three compute a different one, and they never rejoin.

Density eventually leaves its golden curve, while the other signals stay normal. More importantly, the density reading no longer matches the value predicted from the other instruments. When we leave the densitometer out and recompute the relationships, the remaining three agree again. One instrument is out of step while the others still tell a coherent story. That points to the instrument, not the beer.

What part one has shown

That is all part one needs: examples of good batches, a reference curve for each signal, and relationships among the instruments. The method can warn that a batch is drifting and can separate a process problem from a sensor problem. But notice what it never reconstructs: the sugar, alcohol or active yeast themselves. It recognises patterns in the readings without recovering the hidden state that produced them.

Part two · Understanding

Recovering the world's hidden variables

In the brewery, the quantities we cared about most — sugar, alcohol and active yeast — were exactly the ones we did not observe continuously. Part one worked around them. It used the visible signals to detect trouble without reconstructing what was happening inside. Part two asks the harder question: can we take the measurements and recover the hidden variables themselves?

Prediction still matters; part one showed why. But accurate prediction alone does not tell us that a model has recovered the variables that generated the observations. The result in the Klindt, LeCun and Balestriero paper is about this stronger kind of recovery: under particular conditions, a representation learned from observations can line up with the hidden state of the system, up to a rotation.

So we make the problem deliberately clean. Each system has a small set of true hidden variables — quantities a lab could in principle report — and ten sensors that respond to them. We hide the true variables, give the method only the ten sensor readings, and ask what representation it learns. Because we built the system ourselves, we can then compare that representation with the hidden truth.

It helps to see the setup in numbers. In the tablet-press example, the hidden state has four quantities, while the probe returns ten absorbance readings. Below are four rows from the 24,000 simulated samples: the first four columns are the hidden truth in mg per tablet, and the last three are a sample of the ten near-infrared sensor readings.

During training, those four hidden columns are sealed away. The method sees only the ten sensors. The question in every experiment is the same: how much of the hidden state can we recover from what the method learns?

[[ FIGURE 05 — drag medium/figures/fig05.png here ]]

Every experiment has the same shape: a few hidden variables we want, ten sensors we get, and a method — PCA or LeJEPA — to read the ten back into the few, up to a rotation.

Before comparing methods, we ask two questions about each system:

Is the measurement linear? Plot a sensor against the hidden variable that drives it. A straight relationship is linear; a curved one is not. Are the hidden variables Gaussian? Plot their distributions and compare them with a bell curve. A roughly symmetric bell is Gaussian; a skewed or heavy-tailed shape is not.

These questions refer to two different parts of the problem. Linear describes how the sensors respond to the hidden state. Gaussian describes the distribution of the hidden variables themselves. The paper's identifiability result depends on the second; our examples let us see how both properties affect recovery in practice.

Why would a hidden variable be Gaussian at all?
When a quantity is held at a target and only wobbles, those wobbles are the sum of many tiny independent nudges, and they pile up symmetrically into a bell. That is the central limit theorem, and it is what a well-controlled, well-mixed process looks like. A variable comes out not Gaussian when something breaks that symmetry: a hard floor at zero (a concentration can never go negative), multiplicative growth instead of additive wobble, or rare large events. Those give the lopsided, long-tailed shapes we will see in systems three and four.

How the two methods work: biggest change against slowest change

A useful first approximation is this: PCA looks for directions of large variation. It does not use the order of the samples; shuffle time and PCA gives the same answer. LeJEPA uses temporal slowness: neighbouring moments are encouraged to have nearby representations, while additional constraints keep the representation from collapsing.

That last part matters. If the only goal were to change as little as possible, the easiest representation would be a constant — perfectly slow, but useless. LeJEPA prevents that collapse by also shaping the representation globally. In the setting studied by the paper, this combination of local slowness and global regularisation is what makes recovery possible.

Readers familiar with signal processing may recognise the connection to Slow Feature Analysis. The paper builds on that intuition, then adds the conditions needed for an identifiability result: an expressive encoder, a particular regularisation of the representation, and assumptions on how the hidden variables evolve.

Concretely, PCA can only form linear combinations of the ten sensor readings. It searches for a few directions that explain the most variance and uses those as its representation. LeJEPA instead learns an encoder, so the mapping from sensors to representation can be nonlinear. During training it never sees the true concentrations; it is guided only by the objective imposed on neighbouring moments and on the overall representation.

We also train a supervised read-out as a control. Unlike LeJEPA, this model is shown the true hidden variables during training and learns to predict them from the sensors. It is not an unsupervised world-model learner. Its purpose is simpler: to tell us whether the information is present in the measurements at all. If even the supervised model cannot recover a variable, no unsupervised method should be expected to reconstruct it from the same sensors.

To score recovery, we compare each learned representation with the hidden variables on held-out data. The paper's guarantee is only up to an orthogonal transformation, so the coordinates need not appear in the same orientation as the original variables. For the main comparison we use a linear probe and report R² for the worst-recovered hidden variable; in the saccharimeter case we also run the stricter test the theorem actually promises — recovery under an orthogonal map only (rotation or reflection, via Procrustes) — and LeJEPA still reaches R² = 0.98 with QᵀQ = I, so “up to a rotation” is literally earned there.

The LeJEPA box shows the simplified second-moment (covariance) form of the Gaussian constraint used in our compact encoder; the paper's SIGReg matches the full distribution to an isotropic Gaussian.

Keep one contrast in mind as we move through the four systems: PCA is limited to linear directions in the measurements, while LeJEPA can learn a nonlinear encoder and uses the continuity of the system over time.

System 1 · Tablet press (linear, Gaussian)

On a pharmaceutical line, tablets are pressed continuously, and each one must contain the right formulation. The hidden state of the powder blend — active ingredient, filler, lubricant and moisture — matters directly to product quality. Instead of stopping the process for constant laboratory assays, a near-infrared probe can watch the blend as it flows and provide ten optical measurements.

Hidden variables: API (the active drug), lactose, stearate lubricant and moisture. Measurements: absorbance at ten infrared wavelengths. Relationship: in this simulation, Beer–Lambert behaviour makes each absorbance a weighted sum of the concentrations: A = Σ(absorptivity × concentration).

[[ FIGURE 07 — drag medium/figures/fig07.png here ]]

All ten sensors are dead straight (R² = 1.000). A sum of concentrations is exactly linear.

[[ FIGURE 08 — drag medium/figures/fig08.png here ]]

The four ingredient amounts vary batch to batch as tidy bells (|skew| up to 0.06).

This case is linear and Gaussian. The sensor response is linear by construction, while each ingredient fluctuates around a controlled recipe target. It is the easiest corner of our grid: simple measurements and well-behaved hidden variables.

System 2 · Two-sugar saccharimeter (nonlinear, Gaussian)

Our second system is a two-sugar mixture of sucrose and fructose. Both sugars rotate polarized light, and the amount of rotation depends on concentration. A saccharimeter turns that optical effect into measurements from which we would like to recover the two concentrations.

Hidden variables: sucrose and fructose concentrations. Measurements: ten detector signals, from five wavelengths viewed through two polarizing filters. Relationship: concentration changes the angle of polarization, while each detector reads a cos²(angle) response. Once the angle wraps far enough, different concentrations can produce the same reading.

[[ FIGURE 09 — drag medium/figures/fig09.png here ]]

Hold one sugar fixed and raise the other: every detector oscillates up and down as the light keeps turning past the filter. No straight line fits any of it (R² = 0.0001).

So this case is strongly nonlinear, and the important feature is a fold: the measurement is no longer one-to-one.

[[ FIGURE 10 — drag medium/figures/fig10.png here ]]

Yet the two sugar concentrations themselves are clean bells (|skew| 0.09).

The hidden sugar concentrations themselves, however, are Gaussian in our simulation: each stays near a target concentration and drifts around it. That gives us the most interesting corner of the grid — a Gaussian hidden world seen through a nonlinear, folding instrument.

System 3 · River-water metals (ICP-OES) (linear, not Gaussian)

A river-monitoring station may track dissolved metals such as iron, manganese, zinc and calcium. An emission spectrometer excites the sample in a plasma, and the resulting light at characteristic wavelengths carries information about the concentrations. Here we use ten emission lines to recover four metal concentrations.

Hidden variables: iron, manganese, zinc and calcium concentrations. Measurements: ten emission lines. Relationship: the simulated emission intensity is proportional to concentration, so the sensor mapping is linear.

[[ FIGURE 11 — drag medium/figures/fig11.png here ]]

Every line is straight (R² = 1.000). This is linear.

[[ FIGURE 12 — drag medium/figures/fig12.png here ]]

But the metal levels are not bells: piled up at the left, with a long tail to the right (skew up to 5.95, kurtosis — the weight of the tail — up to 76.54).

The hidden variables are not Gaussian. Concentrations cannot fall below zero, and in our simulation they spend much of their time near a low background with occasional multiplicative spikes from discharge-like events. The result is a skewed, long-tailed distribution rather than a bell curve.

System 4 · Anaerobic digester (nonlinear, not Gaussian)

The fourth system is an anaerobic digester: a warm, sealed tank where microbes turn organic material into biogas. Its health depends on hidden quantities such as volatile acids, alkalinity, ammonia and biomass, while the operator sees easier online measurements such as gas flow, methane fraction, pH and redox potential.

Hidden variables: volatile acids, alkalinity, ammonia and biomass. Measurements: ten online probes. Relationship: the simulation includes Haldane/Andrews-style inhibition, so increasing acid can first raise biogas production and then suppress it. The response therefore bends back on itself.

Most probes bend, and the biogas panel actually turns over: it rises, then falls (worst straight-line R² = 0.724).

The acids sit low most of the time and occasionally shoot up (skew up to 3.50, kurtosis up to 17.59 (kurtosis measures how heavy the tail is)).

The hidden variables are also non-Gaussian in this example. Acid can build rapidly during a load upset, producing a skewed, heavy-tailed distribution. This gives us the hardest corner of the grid: nonlinear measurements and non-Gaussian hidden variables.

The test: can we get the hidden variables back?

Now we can test recovery. Each method receives only the ten sensor readings and is evaluated on fresh data against hidden variables it never saw during unsupervised training. We compare PCA with LeJEPA, and we keep the supervised model as a ceiling on how much information the sensors contain.

[[ FIGURE 15 — drag medium/figures/fig15.png here ]]

PCA against LeJEPA on the four systems.

System           Corner                   PCA    LeJEPA  Verdict
---------------  -----------------------  -----  ------  ---------------------------------------
1 Tablet press   linear, Gaussian         0.980  0.980   A tie: linear is enough
2 Saccharimeter  nonlinear, Gaussian      −0.09  0.97    LeJEPA wins outright
3 River metals   linear, not Gaussian     0.993  0.993   A tie: linear is enough
4 Digester       nonlinear, not Gaussian  0.917  0.712   PCA ahead (outside the Gaussian regime)

The linear cases are unsurprising: PCA already recovers the tablet and river-metal states well, so the learned encoder has little room to help. The dramatic difference appears in the saccharimeter. There PCA is essentially unable to recover the two sugars, while LeJEPA reaches about R² = 0.97 in our experiment. The digester is different again: our compact LeJEPA implementation performs worse than PCA there (0.712 against 0.917). That is not a contradiction of the paper: this system sits outside the theorem's Gaussian regime, and our encoder is a compact stand-in rather than the paper's full SIGReg — so this experiment says nothing against LeJEPA on such systems, and the paper's own R² > 0.999 figures, which come from separate idealised benchmarks, should not be transferred here.

Inside system 2, where LeJEPA changes the game

Why does PCA fail on the saccharimeter? Because the measurement folds. As concentration changes, the polarization angle moves forward, but the detector sees cos²(angle). The signal therefore rises, falls and rises again. Different hidden states can land on similar raw readings, winding the two-dimensional hidden state into a more complicated shape.

[[ FIGURE 16 — drag medium/figures/fig16.png here ]]

The spiral: scrambled into a ring by PCA, and unwound by LeJEPA — the true pinwheel, merely turned.

How to read the four panels
Each panel is a simple scatter, and the two axes change from one to the next: the two true sugar concentrations in the first, two of the ten detector readings in the second, and the two recovered coordinates in the last two. Ignore the numbers on the axes — everything is centred and scaled — and watch the shape and the colour instead.
The colour is the key. Every dot is coloured by where the two true concentrations place it on a colour wheel, so the first panel, the hidden truth, is a smooth pinwheel: neighbouring concentrations, neighbouring colours. The second panel is what the raw detectors report; Malus's law has smeared those colours all across the square, high and low concentrations sitting on top of one another.
The last two panels are the reconstructions, each first turned to line up with the truth as well as it can — we allow that rotation, because a rotation is all the theory promises to recover. PCA winds the samples onto nested rings, and no turn of a ring brings the wheel back, so its colours stay scrambled (R² ≈ 0). LeJEPA, given the same freedom, snaps back into the original pinwheel — the same colour wheel as the first panel, merely rotated (R² = 0.97). Watching that wheel reappear is watching “recovery up to a rotation.”
Method               What it is                               Recovery (R²)
-------------------  ---------------------------------------  ----------------------------------------------------------
PCA                  Linear, straight-line directions         −0.09 — worse than the constant-mean baseline
A flexible read-out  Nonlinear, but told the answers          0.99 — strong evidence the information is recoverable here
LeJEPA               Learned encoder, never told the answers  0.97 — recovers it, up to a rotation

PCA cannot undo that nonlinear wrapping with a linear projection. The supervised control reaches about 0.99, which tells us the sugar information is still present in the full set of sensor readings; it has not been destroyed. LeJEPA, trained without those labels, recovers a representation that can be aligned with the two hidden concentrations at about 0.97.

The important point is the geometry of the result. LeJEPA does not necessarily return 'sucrose' on one axis and 'fructose' on the other. Instead, the recovered representation can be a rotated version of the true hidden state. That is exactly the ambiguity allowed by the theorem. Our experiment illustrates the phenomenon; the paper provides the proof and the controlled tests that isolate the role of the Gaussian assumption.

This kind of wrapping is not unique to a saccharimeter. Phase measurements in radar and optics also require unwrapping, and many sensing problems map different physical states to similar observations. The example is simple, but the measurement problem it represents is real.

The PCA-versus-LeJEPA contrast is useful here, but it should not be pushed too far. In the linear systems, PCA already has the right kind of model. In the saccharimeter, it does not: no linear combination of the raw detectors can simply unfold the wrapped geometry. A nonlinear encoder has the flexibility to do that, while the temporal objective provides information about which nearby observations should remain nearby in representation space.

But sometimes even PCA handles a curve

The word nonlinear covers many different situations. A curved response does not automatically require a learned encoder. The more practical question is whether a simple transformation can make the relationship usable again.

If a response is monotone — it keeps moving in one direction — a known transform can sometimes straighten it. In our tests, for example, a multiplicative sensor leaves PCA at 0.80 in raw form and reaches 1.00 after a logarithm. Gentle low-order nonlinearities also remain surprisingly recoverable with a linear probe. In such cases, a simple preprocessing step plus PCA may be entirely adequate.

A fold is harder because the same measurement value can correspond to different hidden states. In the saccharimeter test, raw values, logarithms and low-order polynomial features all leave PCA near zero (−0.15). The nonlinear encoder, by contrast, can learn a representation that separates the wrapped states.

A practical rule of thumb. A smooth, one-way nonlinearity may be handled by a known transform and a simple linear method. A folding, many-to-one measurement is a different problem: a linear projection cannot generally unwrap it, so a nonlinear representation learner becomes much more relevant.

What part two has shown

Across these four controlled systems, the practical lesson is not that LeJEPA always beats PCA. It does not. When the measurements are already linear, PCA is a strong baseline even when the hidden variables are non-Gaussian. The interesting case is the nonlinear, Gaussian saccharimeter: there the linear baseline fails while LeJEPA recovers the hidden state up to the allowed transformation. That example makes the paper's identifiability result easier to picture without pretending our small benchmark is the theorem itself.

The mathematics is in the paper

The examples above are illustrations. The guarantee itself is in the original paper. Under the assumptions studied there — including independent Gaussian latent variables evolving in a stationary setting, an expressive encoder, and optimisation of the stated objective — the authors prove recovery of the hidden variables up to an orthogonal transformation. They also formalise parts of the result in Lean 4.

When Does LeJEPA Learn a World Model? — David Klindt, Yann LeCun & Randall Balestriero, arXiv:2605.26379. Code, proofs and demo: github.com/klindtlab/lejepa-identifiability.

If these examples made linear, Gaussian and up to a rotation easier to picture, the paper is where the mathematical argument begins.

Two scope points matter. First, the theorem is not specifically about chemistry or even about images; it concerns hidden variables behind observations. Our small sensor systems are useful because we can draw and inspect them, but the motivation is broader: in high-dimensional data such as video, spectra or large sensor arrays, the useful representation itself may need to be learned. Second, recovering a hidden state is only one part of a world model. A fuller system also needs dynamics — including what happens under actions — so that the representation can support prediction and planning.

That is the window I wanted these examples to open. The interesting question is not only whether a model can predict what comes next, but whether its internal representation recovers something stable and meaningful about the world that produced the observations. In the brewery we predicted useful signals without reconstructing the chemistry. In the saccharimeter, under the paper's favourable conditions, a learned representation recovered the hidden variables up to a rotation. That gap — between matching observations and recovering the state behind them — is the idea I wanted to make visible.

Files, data and reproducibility

Every parameter, tolerance, fitted coefficient and reported score in this article comes from the executed notebooks in the companion repository. All runs are seeded and deterministic; the saccharimeter result is stable across training seeds, and the notebooks re-run any single score in minutes.

Everything ships in the companion repository, and the whole pipeline is packaged as two executed Jupyter notebooks — readable on GitHub with every figure embedded, or runnable in the browser on Google Colab with nothing to install:

01_prediction_beer_tank.ipynb — the fermentation simulator, both fits (golden curves and cross-instrument reconstruction), the tolerances and the decision rule, and the Tank A / Tank B verdicts. 02_understanding_four_systems.ipynb — the four simulators (system 2 = the Malus-law saccharimeter), the linearity and Gaussianity measurements, PCA against the supervised read-out and the trained LeJEPA encoder, the spiral figure, the orthogonal (Procrustes) check, and the log/polynomial/wrap rescue tests.

The notebooks are fully self-contained: run top to bottom, they generate every dataset themselves (the good tanks, the fault tanks, all four sensor worlds), export the CSVs as they run, and write out every fitted relationship — from the density plane to the per-sensor linear laws (extracted_relationships.txt, sensor_linear_fits.csv). Every number quoted in this article can be regenerated from them in minutes.
